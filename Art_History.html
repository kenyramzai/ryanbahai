My favorite Baha'i history writers

    The purpose of this article is not to even attempt to cover Baha’i history, which would be volumes and volumes. Rather, it is to introduce my favorite writers thereof. For me, the history of the Baha’i Faith is the best part of it all.

    No Baha’i history I know of has been written by official historians. As a matter of fact, the best Baha’i history books have been written by people who brought in a unique perspective due to their background. What we term so called “historians” in the Baha’i Faith are early believers who witnessed events, or spoke to witnesses, or took down people’s recollections of their lives.

    My favorite Baha’i history writers are Nabil, Haji Mirza Haydar Ali, Shoghi Effendi (The Guardian), Ruyyih Khanum (Shoghi Effendi’s wife), Balyuzi, and Adib Taherzadeh. Well? Yeah, that’s 6. I actually wrote their names in Chronological order.

    Nabil was only 13 years old when he heard about 313 guys fighting for their lives against 10,000 soldiers. At 13, he was ready to go fight with them. This was the battle of Fort Tabarsi that lasted an entire year. Nabil arrived a day after the final massacre. He spoke to witnesses.

    His whole life was like this: He either witnessed events himself or spoke to witnesses. So he became an early historian of the Faith.

    He wrote 2 volumes, covering in detail the ministry of the Bab, and the Ministry of Baha’u’llah. Only the first book is translated into English. It is called, “The Dawn Breakers”. Baha’u’llah approved the first volume. After Baha’u’llah died, Nabil got Abdu’l-Baha’s approval of the second volume. Then, he could no longer live without Baha’u’llah, and he committed suicide. He waited until sunset, and threw himself off a cliff into the ocean and rocks. (Is that a poetic suicide?) However, Nabil was one of Baha’u’llah’s 19 apostles in His lifetime. And at least Nabil finished his second volume before killing himself.

    The next guy I love is Haji Mirza Haydar Ali-i-Isfahani. This is actually my favorite guy in all the Baha’i Faith.

    But first, let me explain the long name. His name is Haydar Ali. Mirza before a name means the family job of a scribe, someone who can read and write not just for nobility, but for the every day person. In the old days, not many people could read and write, and the job was passed from father to son. Also, Mirzas were usually involved in the education of children. (Mirza, after the name, is a prince, royal blood).

    Haji is the title given to someone who has been on pilgrimage. For Muslims, this is Mecca, in Saudi Arabia. But for Baha’is, it is at the Baha’i world Center (BWC), current day Israel, ancient day Palestine. Note that the original Baha’i pilgrimage was to the house of the Bab in Shiraz, where he declared, and to the garden of Ridvan (paradise) where Baha’u’llah openly declared his Faith. The house in question was destroyed by enemies of the Faith, and the garden was also taken by enemies. The remains of the Bab and of Baha’u’llah are at the Baha’i World Center. Those are now the places of pilgrimage.

    Now as for words in Persian, names that end in the letter “i”, pronounced “ee” in Persian, it means “comes from”. It applies both to being a descendant of someone (family name) and the location one is born.

    So back to the long name, Haji (Finished Pilgrimmage) Mirza (Scribe) Haydar Ali (Name) - i (descendant of Ali) - Isfahani (of the city of Isfahan).

    Now on to why I like this guy so much.

    Haji Mirza Haydar Ali was pretty much associated with all early Baha’i history. He became a believer at the time of the Bab, defended the Faith against Muslim clergy, became a loyal follower of Baha’u’llah, was imprisoned several times for teaching the Faith, he hated Baha’u’llah’s half-brother from the start (a guy who eventually tried to kill Baha’u’llah with a deadly poison), he later defended Abdu’l-Baha and was one of the “Lions of the Covenant”, and he was one of the very few assigned to watch over Shoghi Effendi as a child, and he was the child’s children class teacher (Sunday school).

    Haji Mirza Haydar Ali left a detailed memoir of his life as a Baha’i. This was upon Abdu’l-Baha’s request. I’m not sure of the name in English. I think it is called “On Wings of the Spirit”. (I read it 25 years ago while at the BWC.) The Persian name is “Bihjatu’s-Sudur”. (But I don’t read or speak Persian.)

    Note: Shoghi Effendi was secretly chosen to be the Guardian at the mere age of 9! But the child’s identity had to be secret, for his safety from enemies and jealous people also. Haji Mirza Haydar Ali (of Isfahan) told Abdu’l-Baha, “But the child is not a fighter!”. Abdu’l-Baha replied, “If you look into the deep brown eyes of my grandson, you will see in them the loyalty he has for the Blessed Beauty”. (The Blessed Beauty was one of 3 expressions commonly used by Baha’is to refer to Baha’u’llah.)

    Note that the name my parents chose for me, Ryan, was the code name for the child that was used in letters. It is also a name that exists in English, Arabic, and Persian.

    My next favorite Baha’i history writer is Shoghi Effendi, as an adult. Well, Shoghi Effendi claimed he was not a historian, but he was one of the best. He could make sense of 10 years of Baha’i history in a single paragraph, all the while adding perspective to it.

    His famous history volume is called, “God Passes By”. It covers the time of the Bab, Baha’u’llah and Abdu’l-Baha, a time period known as “The Heroic Age” of the Baha’i Faith.

    His wife, Ruyyih Khanum (A Canadian) outlived him by about 50 years. She traveled the world, and talked about things she saw all around the world in her book, “Prescription for Living”, such as love and marriage, death, work, habit, sorrow and trial, pursuit of happiness being the pursuit of virtues, the need for a perfect example. She also gives, in that book, a relatively concise but well written history of both the Bab and Baha’u’llah. I translated half of this book into French, giving it my full time attention for about a year.

    In other accounts and books, she wrote very poetically and from her heart about Baha’i events. Her book on her husband, Shoghi Effendi (The Guardian) is called, “The Priceless Pearl”.

    The next Baha’i history writer I like is Balyuzi. He had a Masters in Socio-Economics. And when he analyzes Baha’i history, he gives the social and economic context of the Baha’is, of the city they were in, of the enemies of the Faith and people in power. Then he gives his historical account, with his sources.

    My favorite book from him is called, “Baha’u’llah, the King of Glory”. There is a chapter where he described an exile on foot from Baghdad to Constantinople, then by boat to Adrianople (Istanbul). He gives every stop that took place, and how the small towns received Baha’u’llah as a king, what happened, who had what job during the caravan trip, the order or march, etc. The chapter is called, “The March of the King of Glory”, an exile meant to humiliate Baha’u’llah but that flipped 180 degrees and showed Him a king. And I swear, this guy did his homework.

    Well, “last but not least” definitely applies here. My all-time favorite Baha’i author is this one: Adib Taherzadeh. This guy covered all the Holy Scripture, giving all the context, what was going on in Baha’u’llah’s life, who the stuff was written for, that person’s story, what the enemies of the Faith were doing, and where the books are concerned, the main themes and coolest Baha’i discussions you’ll ever read.

    The guy was an engineer, but his dad was an early historian of the Faith. However, the guy was also a member of the guidance branch of the Faith, someone we call a “Counsellor”. The guy eventually got elected on the world council (The Universal House of Justice), where he served 12 years, until he died.

    His 4 volume master piece is, “The Revelation of Baha’u’llah”, a series dedicated to Baha’i pioneers (missionaries) and travel teachers all over the world. Context is everything, it avoids misrepresentations, it avoids “splits” in the future, avoids war! If you read, “Revelation of Baha’u’llah”, you’ll know about all Baha’u’llah’s books and you’ll be able to not only participate in any Baha’i discussion, but defend your point of vu fully.

    This guy really did his homework on what he talks about. For example, the remains of the Bab were hidden after his execution, and over 60 years moved from hiding place to hiding place – almost discovered and destroyed, many times – until the remains were placed and buried on Mount Carmel, Haifa, Israel, Baha’i World Center. Adib Taherzadeh backtracked every location the body went in those 60 years, who knew the secret at which point, exactly how it was hidden, how the remains were maintained… It’s only a small example, but the guy did his homework. And that says nothing of his mind-blowing discussions.

    He wrote 3 other famous books, “Trustees of the Merciful”, “The Covenant”, and his last book finished right before he died, “The Child of the Covenant”.

    Well, that’s it for my favorite Baha’i authors of history. I also want to get my hands on Martha Root’s diary. She was an American woman who traveled the world 4 times, teaching the Faith wherever adventure led her, and corresponding with Abdu’l-Baha and then Shoghi Effendi. She left a cool diary for posterity.

      Ryan Kazemi
      September 2020
